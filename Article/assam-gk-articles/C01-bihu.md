**Bihu** is not one festival but three — a triple celebration tied to Assam's farming calendar and observed by Assamese people of every faith and community [1] [2]. **Rongali Bihu** (April) marks the New Year and the sowing season, **Kongali Bihu** (October) the anxious growing months, and **Bhogali Bihu** (January) the harvest feast. ADRE treats Bihu as a core culture topic: the months, the ritual days, the foods, and the dance are all frequent questions — and the Bihu dance's recognition on UNESCO's Intangible Cultural Heritage list (2024) makes it current-affairs fresh too [1] [3].

## Rongali Bihu: The Great New Year

**Bohag Bihu**, called **Rongali** ("festival of happiness"), begins around 14–15 April and heralds the Assamese New Year [1] [2]. The seven-day observance traditionally moves through distinct days: **Goru Bihu** (honouring cattle), **Manuh Bihu** (the day of people, with new clothes and greetings), **Kutum/Senehi** (kinship and visits), and **Chera Bihu** (farewell) [1]. The signature expression is the **Bihu dance** — young men and women in red-and-white dress dancing in circles with brisk hip-and-foot movements, while *bihuwans* sing songs of love, nature, and the fields, accompanied by the **dhol, pepa, gogona, taal, and baanhi** [1] [2]. In 2024, the Bihu dance was inscribed on **UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity** — a landmark recognition of Assam's festive tradition [3].

## Kongali Bihu: The Festival of Scarcity

**Kati Bihu**, called **Kongali** ("poor" — the season when granaries run low and crops still stand in the field), falls in October [1] [2]. There is no feast or dance: instead, households light **akaxi gonga** (sky lamps) and **saki** lamps at the base of the tulsi plant and in paddy fields, praying for crop protection and the health of family members working far from home [1] [2]. The gentle **Tuloni bihu** songs sung by children complete a festival whose theme — hopeful austerity — is as much a part of Assamese culture as Rongali's exuberance.

## Bhogali Bihu: The Harvest Feast

**Magh Bihu**, called **Bhogali** ("the month of eating and enjoyment"), comes in January at the end of the harvest [1] [2]. Its eve — **Uruka** — is a community feast night when temporary **bhelaghar** (thatched huts) are erected and food is cooked through the night. At dawn, bonfires called **meji** — built from bamboo, hay, and thatch — are lit, and participants throw **pitha** and **laru** into the flames, offer prayers, and share the feast of **jalpan** (the festive breakfast of chira, doi, pitha, and laru) [1] [2]. The next morning's **tekeli bhonga** — the ritual breaking of earthen pots — closes the celebrations. Bhogali is Assam at its most communal: the meji, the bhelaghar, and the shared table express a culture built around food and togetherness [2].

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many types of Bihu are there?

Three: Rongali (Bohag) Bihu in April, Kongali (Kati) Bihu in October, and Bhogali (Magh) Bihu in January [1] [2].

### What does Rongali Bihu celebrate?

The Assamese New Year and the onset of spring and sowing — seven days moving from Goru Bihu (cattle) through Manuh Bihu (people) to Chera Bihu (farewell) [1] [2].

### What is unique about Kongali Bihu?

It is a festival of scarcity with no feast — households light earthen lamps (akaxi gonga and saki) for crop protection and family welfare [1] [2].

### What are meji and bhelaghar in Bhogali Bihu?

Meji are the dawn bonfires of bamboo and hay; bhelaghar are the temporary thatched huts where the community feasts through the Uruka night [1] [2].

### What instruments accompany the Bihu dance?

The dhol, pepa (buffalo-horn pipe), gogona (bamboo jaw harp), taal (cymbals), and baanhi (flute) [1] [2].

### Has Bihu received international recognition?

Yes — the Bihu dance was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2024 [3].
