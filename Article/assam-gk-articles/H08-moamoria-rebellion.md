The **Moamoria Rebellion (1769–1805)** was the internal earthquake that ended six centuries of Ahom stability. A rising of followers of the **Mayamara (Moamaria) satra** against the Ahom state — fuelled by the oppressive Paik system and the growing political clout of the satras — it killed roughly **half the kingdom's population**, destroyed the economy, and left Assam weak enough for the Burmese invasions and, ultimately, the Treaty of Yandabo [1]. For ADRE, it is the bridge topic between the Ahom chapter and the colonial chapter, and its dates and consequences are asked regularly.

## The Background: Satra, Paik, and State

Two forces were on collision course in the late 18th-century Ahom kingdom. The first was the **Paik system**: every adult male owed rotational state labour or militia service, a burden that grew heavier as the state's demands outpaced the economy [1]. The second was the **satra movement**: Vaishnavite monasteries offered spiritual refuge and, crucially, an escape from Paik obligations — followers of a satra were withdrawn from state service. The royalist satras (of other Ekasarana samhatis) supported the kings; the **Mayamara satra**, belonging to the non-conformist **Kala-samhati** sect and followed largely by **Moran** communities (the backbone of the Ahom militia), became a rival power centre [1].

## The Course of the Rebellion

The rebellion broke out in **1769** during the reign of **Sunyeophaa**, beginning with the killing of a royal officer and escalating into open war. The Moamoria forces repeatedly seized the capital — twice the Ahom king lost Garhgaon itself — and each reconquest by the royal forces, led by ministers like **Purnanada Burhagohain**, was accompanied by massacres that depopulated whole tracts of the kingdom [1]. By the rebellion's close around 1805, under **Suklingphaa (Kamaleswar Singha)**, the state had "won" at a catastrophic cost: traditional accounts hold that about **half the kingdom's population perished** through death and flight, and the economy was ruined [1].

## The Consequences: Matak Rajya and the Road to Yandabo

The kingdom that emerged was a shadow. The north-eastern region of **Bengmara** (modern Tinsukia district) became the **Matak Rajya** — a tribute-paying but virtually independent territory ruled by **Sarbananda Singha** under the new office of **Borsenapati** [1]. The Paik-based militia was effectively dead, replaced by hired Hindustani sepoys. When the **Burmese invaded in 1817**, the hollowed-out kingdom offered little resistance; three invasions later (1817–1826), the British intervened and the **Treaty of Yandabo (24 February 1826)** ended Ahom sovereignty [2]. In a real sense, Yandabo was the Moamoria Rebellion's final invoice.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What was the Moamoria Rebellion?

An uprising (1769–1805) of followers of the Mayamara satra against the Ahom monarchy, caused by Paik system oppression and satra-state rivalry; it devastated the kingdom [1].

### When did the Moamoria Rebellion begin?

In 1769, during the reign of Swargadeo Sunyeophaa; it continued intermittently until about 1805, during Suklingphaa's reign [1].

### Who were the Moamorias?

Followers of the Moamaria (Mayamara) satra of the Kala-samhati sect, predominantly Morans but including Chutias, Sonowal Kacharis, artisans, and some Ahom officers [1].

### What were the main causes of the rebellion?

Oppressive Paik system obligations, the satras draining manpower from state service, and the political rivalry between the non-conformist Kala-samhati sect and the royalist satras [1].

### What was the Matak Rajya?

The semi-independent territory in north-eastern Assam (Bengmara, modern Tinsukia) created after the rebellion, ruled by Sarbananda Singha as Borsenapati [1].

### How did the Moamoria Rebellion lead to British rule?

It destroyed the kingdom's population and economy, leaving it unable to resist the Burmese invasions of 1817–1826, which ended with the Treaty of Yandabo ceding Assam to the British [1] [2].
