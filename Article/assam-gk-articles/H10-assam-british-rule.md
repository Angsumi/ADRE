The 121 years between the **Treaty of Yandabo (1826)** and Indian independence (1947) transformed Assam more than any earlier era: British policy created its **tea economy**, nearly erased then revived its **language**, gave it a separate **provincial identity in 1874**, and provoked the **freedom movement** that ended the Raj [1] [2]. This guide covers every British-era milestone ADRE asks about — dates, firsts, personalities, and turning points — in exam-ready form.

## Administration: From Agent to Province

After Yandabo, the British governed through an **Agent of the Governor-General** — Captain **David Scott** was the first, holding court over the last nominal Ahom king Purandar Singha until Upper Assam's annexation in **1838** [1] [2]. Assam was then administered as part of Bengal for four decades — a period of neglect that produced the language crisis of 1836, when **Bengali replaced Assamese** in courts, schools, and offices [2]. The turning point came in **1874**, when Assam became a separate **Chief Commissioner's Province** with **Shillong** as capital; in the same era **Assamese was restored as the official language** following the campaign of Hem Chandra Barua, Anandaram Barooah, and Gunabhiram Barua [2].

## The Tea Economy

Tea is the British era's economic signature. After **Robert Bruce** identified the indigenous tea plant (1823) and his brother **Charles** with **Maniram Dewan** pioneered cultivation, the first commercial plantation was established at **Chabua in 1839** [2]. By the century's end, Assam was the tea heartland of the empire — with plantations that reshaped demography through mass labour migration, and with **Maniram Dewan**, who became the first Assamese tea planter before his execution for the 1857 uprising, as its most complex figure [2].

## The Language Movement and the Jonaki Era

The 1836 imposition of Bengali provoked a cultural resistance that became the great revival of Assamese letters. **Hem Chandra Barua** (the first modern Assamese lexicographer), **Anandaram Barooah**, and **Gunabhiram Barua** campaigned for the mother tongue's restoration; Assamese regained official status in the 1870s, and in **1889** the magazine ***Jonaki*** launched the **Jonaki era** — the literary renaissance carried by Lakshminath Bezbaruah, Hemchandra Goswami, and Chandrakumar Agarwala [2]. The **Assam Sahitya Sabha (1917)** institutionalised the movement. These names and dates are standard ADRE fare.

## Partition, Province, and the March to 1947

The **Partition of Bengal (1905)** attached Assam to East Bengal, creating the **province of Eastern Bengal and Assam** with Dhaka as capital — reversed in **1912**, when Assam again became a Chief Commissioner's province [2]. The freedom movement grew from the **Patharughat Ran (28 January 1894)** peasant firing against land-revenue hikes, through the **Assam Association (1903)** and Congress organisation, to the Quit India heroism of **Kanaklata Barua (1942)** and **Kushal Konwar**, and finally **Gopinath Bordoloi's** premiership (1946), which kept Assam in India despite Muslim League pressure and the **Sylhet referendum of 1947** that transferred Sylhet to East Bengal [3]. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### When did British rule begin in Assam?

With the Treaty of Yandabo on 24 February 1826, after the First Anglo-Burmese War; full annexation of Upper Assam followed in 1838 [1] [2].

### When was Assam made a separate province?

In 1874, as a Chief Commissioner's province with Shillong as its capital [2].

### Why was Bengali imposed in Assam and when removed?

In 1836 the British made Bengali the official language for administrative convenience; sustained protest led to Assamese's restoration in the early 1870s (officially 1873) [2].

### When did tea cultivation start in Assam?

After Robert Bruce's identification of the indigenous plant (1823); the first plantation was established at Chabua in 1839 [2].

### What was the Jonaki era?

The Assamese literary renaissance beginning with the magazine *Jonaki* in 1889, led by Lakshminath Bezbaruah and his contemporaries [2].

### Who was the first Agent of the Governor-General in Assam?

Captain David Scott, appointed after the Treaty of Yandabo to administer the newly acquired region [2].
