If the satra is Assam's monastery, the **namghar** (naamghar) is its every-village extension: the congregational prayer hall at the heart of **Ekasarana Dharma**, created by **Srimanta Sankardeva** and replicated in nearly every Assamese village, town, and diaspora community on Earth [1] [2]. More than a temple, the namghar is a social institution — a courtroom, a cultural stage, and a democratic assembly in one. ADRE asks about its origin, its founder, and its role in society. This guide covers each.

## Origin: Sankardeva's Democratic Temple

Sankardeva's Ekasarana movement needed no priesthood, no images, and no elaborate ritual — only congregational singing of the name of Krishna-Vishnu (**naam-kirtan**) and simple devotion [1] [2]. The namghar was designed as the people's temple: a hall built and maintained collectively by the village, open to all, where devotees gathered to chant, listen to the **Borgeet**, and witness the **Bhaona** performances of the Ankiya Naat [1] [2]. At the centre of every namghar stands the **tulsi plant** — the living symbol of the tradition — before which the congregation faces during prayer [1].

## More Than Prayer: The Social Institution

The namghar's significance in ADRE's social-questions domain lies in its breadth. Historically and today, it functions as the village's **social parliament**: disputes are mediated before the congregation, community decisions (festivals, harvest cooperation, charity) are taken within its walls, and its festivals — from Bihu to Raas — are organised through it [1] [2]. The office-bearers (the **namghar committee**, with the **bhakat** leading worship) operate on elective, accountable principles that scholars have described as a native democratic practice centuries before modern institutions [2]. In this sense, the namghar is where Assamese civil society has always lived.

## Namghar and the Cultural Calendar

Every major Assamese cultural event touches the namghar. The **Borgeet** and **kirtan** are its daily music; **Bhaona** and Sattriya pieces are performed in its courtyard; the **tulsi** is ceremonially honoured; and the community feasts of **Bhogali Bihu** and religious festivals flow through its organisation [1] [2]. Assamese communities from Delhi to Dubai build namghars as their first institution abroad — making it, alongside the gamusa, the most portable symbol of Assamese identity [2].

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is a namghar?

The "House of the Name" — the congregational prayer hall of Assam's Ekasarana Dharma, where devotees chant the divine name together [1] [2].

### Who created the namghar tradition?

Srimanta Sankardeva, as the village-level institution of the Ekasarana (neo-Vaishnavite) movement in the 15th–16th century [1] [2].

### What stands at the centre of a namghar?

The tulsi plant, before which the congregation gathers for nama-kirtan [1].

### What religions tradition does the namghar belong to?

Ekasarana Dharma (neo-Vaishnavism / Namdharma) — devotion to one God through the congregational chanting of Krishna's name [1] [2].

### What social functions does the namghar perform?

Prayer and kirtan, cultural performances (Borgeet, Bhaona), community meetings, dispute mediation, and festival organisation [1] [2].

### Why is the namghar called a democratic institution?

Because it is collectively built, managed by elected committees, open to all villagers, and used for communal decision-making — a native democratic practice [2].
