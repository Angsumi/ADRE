**Sattriya** is Assam's gift to India's classical dance canon: a five-century-old tradition created by **Srimanta Sankardeva** inside the state's Vaishnavite monasteries (satras) as part of devotional theatre, and formally recognised in **2000** as India's **eighth classical dance** by the Sangeet Natak Akademi [1] [2]. For ADRE, Sattriya questions cluster around its founder, its monastic origin, and its 2000 recognition. This guide covers the full story in exam-ready form.

## The Monastery Stage: How Sattriya Was Born

Sankardeva (1449–1568) created the **Ekasarana Dharma** — a devotional Vaishnavite movement — and designed its worship around performance. Within the satras he and his chief disciple **Madhavdeva** established from the late 15th century, devotees enacted the **Ankiya Naat** — Sankardeva's one-act plays on Krishna's life — through stylised **Bhaona** performances, and the dance vocabulary woven into these enactments became Sattriya [1] [2]. Unlike court dances that evolved for royal patrons, Sattriya was born as **worship**: every gesture, costume, and rhythm originally served the glorification of Vishnu-Krishna within the monastery's daily ritual life [1].

## From Satra Ritual to Classical Stage

For centuries Sattriya remained confined to the satras of Majuli, Barpeta, and beyond — a men-only monastic tradition passed through generations of bhakats [1] [2]. The 20th century opened it outward: disciples and scholars adapted the satra repertoire for the proscenium stage, and in **November 2000** the **Sangeet Natak Akademi** granted Sattriya classical status — the first new entry since the original seven, making it **India's eighth classical dance** [1] [2]. Its repertoire today splits into **Nrittya** (pure, abstract dance) and **Nritya** (expressive, narrative dance), with signature pieces like *Krishnar Bhitor Bhor* drawing on the Borgeet tradition [1].

## Sattriya's Creative Family

Sattriya sits at the centre of a creative family Sankardeva founded. The **Borgeet** are his devotional songs — the classical vocal tradition of Assam; the **Ankiya Naat** are the one-act plays performed as Bhaona; and the **mukha shilpa** (mask-making) tradition of Majuli supplies the masks through which the plays come alive [1] [2]. Together these arts — dance, song, drama, and craft — make the satra the most complete cultural institution in Assam, a fact the exam tests through all four doors.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who created Sattriya dance?

Srimanta Sankardeva in the 15th–16th century, as part of devotional performances (Bhaona) in Assam's Vaishnavite satras [1] [2].

### When was Sattriya recognised as a classical dance?

In 2000, by the Sangeet Natak Akademi — making it India's eighth classical dance [1] [2].

### Where did Sattriya originate?

Inside the Vaishnavite monasteries (satras) of Assam — especially Majuli and Barpeta — as ritual worship of Vishnu-Krishna [1] [2].

### What are the two streams of Sattriya?

Nrittya (pure dance) and Nritya (expressive dance) [1].

### What is the connection between Sattriya and Bhaona?

Sattriya developed as the dance element of Bhaona — the performance of Sankardeva's one-act plays (Ankiya Naat) in the satras [1] [2].

### Which other arts belong to Sankardeva's tradition?

Borgeet (devotional songs), Ankiya Naat (one-act plays), and mukha shilpa (mask-making) [1] [2].
