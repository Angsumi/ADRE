Assam's climate is a **tropical monsoon** climate — hot, humid summers; a drenching monsoon; and mild, fog-wrapped winters — and it is the invisible hand behind everything in the state: the tea, the rice, the floods, the festivals, and even Bihu's calendar [1] [2]. ADRE's climate questions are straightforward: the climate type, the seasons, the rainfall range, and the extremes. This guide covers each in exam-ready form.

## The Three Seasons

The year divides cleanly into three. **Summer (March–May)** builds heat and humidity, with maximums reaching 35–37°C in the plains and pre-monsoon storms — the *kal baisakhi*-type thunderstorms — breaking the heat in violent squalls [1] [2]. The **monsoon (June–September/October)** delivers the overwhelming majority of the annual rain; the southwest monsoon winds, forced upward by the Himalayas to the north and the Meghalaya plateau to the south, dump their moisture across the valley [1]. **Winter (November–February)** is mild and famously foggy: minimums dip to about 8–10°C, and Brahmaputra fog shapes transport, agriculture, and the winter Bihu (Magh/Bhogali) traditions [2].

## Rainfall: The State That Drinks the Sky

Assam's rainfall is among India's heaviest, averaging **2,000 to 3,800 mm** annually depending on region — the hill fringes and upper valley receiving the most, the western districts somewhat less [1] [2]. The proximity of the world's rain capitals (Mawsynram and Cherrapunji on the Meghalaya plateau to the south) conditions the whole region's hydrology [1]. The rainfall pattern is the direct engine of the state's agriculture calendar — the *ahu*, *sali*, and *bao* rice crops, the tea flushes, and the jute season all track the monsoon's arrival and withdrawal [2].

## The Double-Edged Sword: Floods and Drought

The same monsoon that feeds the state also drowns it. The Brahmaputra's annual floods — intensified by the river's steep gradient, heavy silt load, and channel shifting — inundate thousands of square kilometres most years, eroding islands like Majuli and displacing lakhs of people [1] [2]. Conversely, dry spells and **winter droughts** affect the Barak Valley and the char populations, and heatwaves have become more frequent in recent decades. Climate change adds a longer arc: glacial retreat in the Brahmaputra's Tibetan headwaters and intensifying rainfall extremes are reshaping the state's risk map [1].

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the climate type of Assam?

Tropical monsoon (humid subtropical in the plains), with hot humid summers, heavy monsoon rains, and mild foggy winters [1] [2].

### How many seasons does Assam have?

Three: summer (March–May), monsoon (June–September/October), and winter (November–February) [1] [2].

### What is the annual rainfall in Assam?

Between about 2,000 and 3,800 mm, with the upper valley and hill fringes receiving the most [1] [2].

### What is the temperature range in Assam?

Approximately 8°C in winter minimums to 35–37°C in summer maximums in the plains [1] [2].

### Why does Assam flood every year?

The Brahmaputra's heavy monsoon discharge, steep gradient, massive silt load, and shifting channels cause annual inundation across the valley [1] [2].

### How does Assam's climate shape its agriculture?

The monsoon calendar governs the three rice crops (ahu, sali, bao), the tea harvest flushes, and the jute season — agriculture in Assam is essentially monsoon agriculture [1] [2].
