The **Treaty of Yandabo**, signed on **24 February 1826** at the village of Yandabo in Burma, is the date on which Assam's medieval era ended and its colonial era began. It concluded the **First Anglo-Burmese War (1824–1826)** and forced Burma to cede **Assam**, along with Manipur, Arakan, and Tenasserim, to the British East India Company [1] [2]. For ADRE, the treaty's date, the war it ended, the territories transferred, and its consequence — the end of Ahom sovereignty — are the four facts that carry the marks [1] [3].

## The Road to Yandabo: Why the British Came

Assam's collapse was home-grown before it was foreign. The **Moamoria Rebellion (1769–1805)** halved the kingdom's population and wrecked its economy, and the desperate late Ahom court invited the **Burmese** in; three Burmese invasions followed between **1817 and 1826**, producing what Assamese tradition calls the **Manor Din** — "the days of the Burmese" — a period of terror and depopulation [1] [4]. The Burmese push west toward Bengal brought them to the borders of British India, and the Company, alarmed, declared war in **1824** [2]. By 1826 British forces had driven the Burmese back to within 70 miles of Ava, and Burma sued for peace [2].

## The Terms of the Treaty

The treaty's territorial clause is what matters for Assam: Burma **renounced all claims to Assam**, and also ceded **Manipur, Arakan (Rakhine), and Tenasserim** to the British [1] [2]. Burma additionally agreed to pay a **war indemnity of one crore rupees** in instalments and to receive a British **resident at Ava** [2]. There was no Assamese signatory — the kingdom that had ruled for 600 years had no seat at the table, which is itself a telling mark of how thoroughly the Moamoria devastation had hollowed it out [4].

## The Aftermath in Assam

**Captain (later Agent) David Scott** was appointed the first Agent of the Governor-General in the North-East and effectively administered Assam through the last nominal Ahom king, **Purandar Singha** (1833–1838). In 1838 the British annexed Upper Assam outright, ending the dynasty; in **1874** Assam became a separate **Chief Commissioner's Province** [3]. The colonial period brought the tea industry (the Bruce brothers' discovery, beginning 1839), railways, and the Bengali-language imposition of 1836 — each a turning point the [Assam under British rule](#) article covers in full.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When was the Treaty of Yandabo signed?

On 24 February 1826, at Yandabo village on the Irrawaddy, ending the First Anglo-Burmese War [1] [2].

### Which war did the Treaty of Yandabo end?

The First Anglo-Burmese War of 1824–1826, fought after the Burmese occupied Assam and threatened Bengal [2].

### Which territories did Burma cede under the treaty?

Assam, Manipur, Arakan, and Tenasserim; Burma also paid a one-crore-rupee indemnity and accepted a British resident at Ava [2].

### How did the treaty affect the Ahom dynasty?

It ended Ahom sovereignty: the British installed Purandar Singha as a tributary king, then annexed Upper Assam in 1838, closing the 600-year dynasty [3] [4].

### Who was the first British administrator of Assam after Yandabo?

Captain David Scott, appointed Agent of the Governor-General in the North-East [3].

### Why is 1826 a turning point in Assam's history?

It marks the end of indigenous rule (Ahom) and the beginning of colonial rule — the hinge between Assam's medieval and modern eras [1] [2].
